This first Report of the John G. Kerr Refuge for In-
sane is sent out with the hope that its story may in-
terest its readers and enlarge its circle of friends. It re-
ceived a shock in the loss of its founder, but has had a
prosperous year under the charge of its faithful superinten-
dent, Charles C. Selden, M.D., Ph.D. While its history
thus far has led those most closely allied to its interests
to thank God and take courage, its wants are
many, and it is by no means able as yet to walk alone.
The most pressing need is a third building. The two we
have can only accommodate fifty patients comfortably.
There are fifty under its roof, and fresh applications constant-
ly coming in. The outcome in meeting current expenses
has been quite satisfactory, although some of the patients
are wholly dependent upon the Refuge for support. This
will doubtless always be the case. " The poor ye have
always with you" is true here as elsewhere. Unfortunately
the price of building material has greatly increased within
the past two years in Canton-in fact nearly doubled, but
we have a sufficient sum on hand to lay the founda-
tions of a new cottage, which will be done without de-
lay, feeling sure that in due time means will be furnished
for the completion of the building. We ask the prayers of
our readers for the well being and success of the Refuge,
that wisdom may be given to its leaders, and that in all
things it may be so conducted that God may be glorified.

OF THE
REFUGE FOR THE INSANE

CANTON.

It is a well known but lamentable fact that throughout
the Chinese empire, during its history of thousands of years,
with its vast population and boasted civilization, there has
never been any arrangements for the insane, except such as
could be made in families, or in prisons. Among the better
classes, confinement in a strong room, often chained to a post,
was all that could be done. With the poor, the unfortunate
lunatic was chained to a heavy stone, or to a post in the
ground, and his fate was to pass months, or years in a close,
dark, and damp room with little attention to comfort, or clean-
liness, and words of kindness seldom sounded in his ears.
There is no doubt but that a short method of getting rid of the
helplessly incurable, has often been resorted to in a country
where the father has the power of life and death over his
family; and among the poor, death is, in numerous instances,
hastened by ill treatment and want of care. With such a
state of things, no one can doubt the necessity of establishing
asylums for the insane.

During the forty-five years I had charge of the Medical
Missionary Society's hospital in Canton, this necessity was
often forced upon me, and in my report to the Society in
1872-73, I brought the condition of this most helpless and de-
pendent class of our fellow-men to the notice of the Society,
from which the following is an extract :-

The establishment of asylums for insane is the only remedy for the
sufferings of this class of people. I have often proposed such an institu-
tion to wealthy Chinese and have urged it upon persons interested in
the native hospitals recently established in Hongkong and in this
city. But it seems altogether unlikely that anything will be done unless

taken in hand, we respectfully and earnestly solicit the aid and support
of the Press, foreign and native, and of officials, business men, and
missionaries, who in their several positions come in contact with and in-

In 1892 I secured a lot of ground in a good location in
the vicinity of Canton, and in order to conciliate the goodwill
of the shopmen in the neighborhood, a dispensary was started
on the ground. While in the United States on furlough in
1892, I was authorized by the Provisional Committee to solicit
funds for the asylum, but owing to causes not necessary to
enumerate here, nothing was accomplished. I returned to
Canton in 1894, just as the bubonic plague was spreading
consternation in that city and Hongkong. Being without
means to go on with the undertaking, the Provisional Commit-
tee was dissolved and the small amount of money in the
treasury was turned over to me.

REFUGE FOR INSANE.

After waiting three years longer, a sum of money was
entrusted to me by a medical missionary residing in the Far
East to be used for medical charity for the Cantonese. With
his consent part of this was used for the erection, in 1897, of a
building on the ground I had purchased in 1892.

A beginning was thus made, and with the funds from the
Provisional Committee and from other sources, I was able to
erect a second building.

In the meantime a part of the ground had been filled in
and progress made in putting up a wall around the grounds.
There are twenty-four rooms in the two buildings, with a ca-
pacity for about fifty inmates. The design was to begin with a
small number, because attendants had to be specially trained
to take care of them. With kind and careful attention, com-
fortable accommodations, pleasant surroundings, good food, and
out-door exercise, it was expected that these sanitary measures
would improve the physical condition and promote mental
improvement. The change from ill treatment and neglect, and
absence of other causes of irritation in the home among igno-
rant relatives and unfeeling servants, will go a long way
towards effecting a cure in many cases.

The first inmate was taken into the refuge in February,
1898. No public notice was given, but in that year thirteen
were received ; during the next year thirty-six, and in 1900
fifty-one, making a total of one hundred. Of these, twenty
have recovered, thirteen have been discharged improved and
ten not improved. Eleven have died, and forty-one remain
under treatment; many of them being much improved. Of the
whole number, seventy were men and thirty women.

In issuing this first Report of the Canton Refuge for
Insane, I wish to place on record the interest taken in the object
by the Rev. E. P. Thwing, then of Brooklyn, New York.
There had been some correspondence with Prof. Thwing on
the subject, and he decided to visit China in the interest of
missionary, philanthropic, and scientific matters he was studying.

During my stay in the United States a leaflet was pre-
pared, giving reasons why asylums for the insane should be
introduced into China. It is as follows :--

First .- In the empire of China, with five times the population of the
United States, there are no asylums for the insane.

Second .- The insane cannot be properly treated or cared for in their

Third .- The insane cannot be received into missionary hospitals or

Fourth .- By reason of poverty, ignorance, inhumanity, carelessness,

missionaries.

A WORD TO WOMEN.
Why they should Aid in Introducing Asylums for the Insane into China.

One half of the insane in China are women. Because of the low
estimate in which women are held in this vast heathen Empire they are
exposed even more than men to the neglect, ill treatment, and suffering
which fall to the lot of persons bereft of reason.

This helpless class of her sex appeals with a force peculiarly strong to
women in a Christian country where, if this direful misfortune comes

The hearts of women run out in sympathy to all who are in distress.

REFUGE FOR INSANE.

In concluding this Editorial of the first Report of the
Refuge for Insane, permit me to say that we would have been
glad if it could have been put into print at an earlier date. But
circumstances over which we had no control have prevented it.
Pioneer work has its difficulties and hindrances, and this
enterprize has been no exception to the general rule. It is only
true to say this has been the most laborious mission work I
have ever undertaken. But during these years of effort in this
direction, when prospects have been dark and obstacles numer-
ous, there has been one woman ever ready with words of cheer,
and her help and encouragement cannot be forgotten. Her
faith in the establishment and success of the institution has
never wavered, and I wish to say here that to my wife the
Refuge in a great measure owes its existence.

I wish to express my sincere thanks to the friends who
have given substantial aid when it was so much needed, and
especially to my fellow medical missionaries in various parts
of China who have shown much interest in this effort to extend
the benefits of Western medicine and Christian benevolence, to
a class of the people hitherto neglected. A beginning has been
made and may it be prospered until the Refuge shall reach and
benefit thousands of the helpless sufferers for whom it was
founded. We thank God and take courage feeling that He
who has hitherto watched over the work will bless it even more
abundantly in the future.

J. G. KERR.

REFUGE FOR INSANE.

Our First Patient.

This man, Wo Cheuk, was the first patient taken into the
Refuge for Insane in Canton, and the first Chinese in the
empire to be received into such an institution. The doctor was
called by the father to see his son, and found him in a damp,
dark, illy-ventilated room at the rear of the house. Windows
there were none, but an opening in the roof prevented the man
from being shut up in total darkness. He was chained by the
ancle, as seen in the photograph, to a large stone mortar, and
thus he had sat upon the floor of this room for three years.
His joints had stiffened from their long confinement, and he
had entirely lost the power to walk. This is what heathenism
does for its most helpless class of sufferers. The poor man
was brought to the Canton hospital, where he remained until
the first building of the Refuge was completed.

On a dark rainy morning in February, 1898, the patient
was taken from his bed and carried on the back of a coolie
down to the landing, where a boat was in readiness to convey
him to the Refuge. Dr. Kerr led the procession of three, the
writer bringing up the rear. We reached the new building which
stood finished and ready for its mission. Its doors opened to
admit the party, and the invalid was made comfortable in a
clean airy room, and henceforth was to know the meaning of
kindness and care.

Then a company of three (another missionary having arrived
a little later) we retired into an unoccupied room, and the insti-
tution for which he had labored and prayed for thirty years,
was dedicated by its founder to God. It was laid at Jesus' feet
to be His and His alone. Thus commended to the love and
care of the Heavenly Father this Refuge for Insane began its
ministry of mercy to the most helpless of all helpless classes.
But the sufferer who had first found a home within its walls,
was beyond recovery. He lingered on a few months and then
passed away.

To our surprise it was discovered soon after the opening
of the Refuge that there was a case of insanity almost within
a stone's throw of it. This woman was found in a shed, with
a chain about her neck, confined by a lock, the other end of
which was fastened to the rickety floor. She had been shut
up alone in this shed for many months.

Her family were boat people, poor, and obliged to work
from dawn until dark for their daily subsistence. They were
persuaded to place her in the Refuge, and it was arranged they
should prepare and bring her food to her, as they did not feel
able to furnish money for her support. For a long time after
her entrance she was as one deaf and dumb. By and by,
however, she began to answer in monosyllables, and little by
little to show an interest in what was going on around her.

It was pathetic to see the joy of the old mother as she
saw signs of improvement in her daughter. The change which
gradually came over the patient was indeed most encouraging.
She came to us seemingly dead to all around her ; she went from

FUNG TAI, THE POLICEMAN.

The picture is an exact reproduction of our "Hercules,"
a sobriquet given him on account of his great strength.

He had been a policeman in the governor's yamên, and
if bodily strength was a requisite for that post, he certainly had
one qualification for it. His whole being seemed to have
become centered in the animal.

Of intellectual and moral faculties he was almost desti-
tute. His bump of destructiveness had developed to abnormal
proportions. To lay his hand upon anything, either animal or
vegetable, and destroy it, was his delight. One day he seized
a long bamboo pole and brandished it about with such vigor as
to keep his attendants at bay. This occurred while he was
out with the other patients taking his daily exercise. The doctor
happened to see him, and soon dispossessed him of his weapon.
His appetite was enormous, and the amount he would consume
was only limited by what was felt wise to give him. At last
he became the victim of wasting disease, to which his herculean
strength finally succumbed.

one treated with kindness and cared for in illness. They

WONG A-FUK.

This patient entered the Refuge July 27th, 1899, at the age
of twenty-five years. The father was a business man in Hong-
kong, and his son had been educated in Victoria College and
spoke English very well. His mind had been disordered for
six months, and when admitted, had taken the form of silent
melancholy. Weeks would pass without his uttering a sound.
He continued in this way for months, when he began to show
displeasure in regard to his food, or whatever was not pleasing
to him. He would make a great disturbance, tear up his
clothes, and become unmanagable.

At such times the doctor seemed the only one who could
quiet him. After about thirteen months he began to show
signs of improvement, and was gradually restored to his right
mind. He was dismissed, cured, March 11th, 1901, and
returned home with his father after a residence of thirteen
and a half months in the Refuge.

FIRST REPORT

Sketches of Dr. Kerr.

We insert here a short sketch written by a former student
of Dr. Kerr's, and for some years one of the instructors of his
medical class. He says :-

In the seventeenth year of the present dynasty I, Chü In-ting, became
a member of Dr. Kerr's medical class. I saw his love for this people and
his joy in explaining the plan of salvation to them. This he felt was
exceedingly important.

Wherever he went among the sick and diseased he never failed to
talk with them about the gospel. He wanted them to belong soul
and body to Christ. Seeing the distress of these sufferers, was as if
he himself suffered. Whether night or day he was always ready to care
for the sick patients. He would often go out two or three times during
the night to look after them. Also would pray with them, asking God to
pardon their sins and relieve their pain. There was not one who did not
love him. I was constantly with him, and seeing his life thus I also
became, the second year of my course, a Christian. Until I finished my
course and went away, he talked a great deal with me and exhorted me.

' If you are going out to be a doctor,' he said, ' your conduct must be
correct and upright, and in the Saviour's name you must do your work.
You will so help your patients into the light. Perhaps you will find your
patients poor and in need. You must give your services to them. Always
have a heart of love for them.' This teaching I cannot attain unto.
When sending out his students, after they had finished their studies, he
gave them his parting counsels. He told them their object must not be
to make money, but to do good ; that they must live Christian lives and
try to help others to be Christians. I saw his great loving heart ; so he
loved the insane. For this reason he built up the Refuge for Insane in
which to receive and cure them. Such an Institution as this China has
but this one. Those who were able to understand had come out each
morning to morning prayers. Those who could, read in his turn a verse
of the chapter, which was afterwards explained by one of the helpers.
We treated them all kindly, and would allow no one to be rough or cross
to them. When he was dangerously ill and near to death, he remembered
these insane as though they were his children. Truly great was his love
for this people, so great we cannot measure it.

Of the American, Dr. J. G. Kerr, this paper is written. In 1861, I, Tso
To-ming, was in San Francisco. I was under the instruction of Dr. Loomis,
of the Presbyterian Mission, who taught me to read the English Bible.
He also baptized me when I joined the church. In the autumn of
1867, Dr. Kerr and family, with Miss H. Noyes, arrived there on their
way to China. They were detained ten days waiting for the sailing of
their steamer for Hongkong.

Dr. Loomis exhorted me to study medicine with Dr. Kerr, in order to
aid the Chinese and also to assist myself. He said the knowledge of
English will help you to do business, but is not necessary for doing
benevolent work. Dr. Kerr was also pleased to receive me as a pupil.
I therefore returned with him to Canton, which we reached after the
voyage of a month. The Dr. went to the hospital at Kuk Fan to live. He
did not find it in a very good condition. There were only two buildings.
The first was two storeys, fronting on the river. In the lower storey, at
either end was a ward for patients. Between them a chapel, where
religious services were held every morning, led by Rev. Charles Preston.

Dr. Kerr and family lived in the upper storey. Behind this build-
ing was a very small one, and behind that an open space, rented as gardens
by the Chinese. The grounds were not enclosed, but the surrounding
houses served as a wall to the premises. Some of these were in a tumble
down condition and did not look very beautiful, so the hospital was
small and not good looking, but large numbers came to be healed. Dr.
Kerr had also another hospital at Kam Si Fan.

Mondays and Fridays were dispensing days at Kuk Fan, Tuesdays
and Saturdays at Kam Si Fan ; Wednesdays and Thursdays there were
vaccinations and surgical operations.

After two years, the Kam Si Fan hospital was closed and all went to
Kuk Fan for treatment.

The patients from the country steadily increased. The room was
not sufficient to receive them, so some had to lie in the verandah, or
about the staircases. This was not very pleasant for the family, but Dr.
Kerr would not on that account refuse to receive them. This condition
lasted some years.

At last the Doctor was able to fill in the frontage and build a house
for himself and family. The rooms he had occupied were then handed over
to the patients. Sometimes there were so many cases of serious illness
and so many surgical operations that the Doctor was obliged to operate
in the evening. He often hardly took time to eat, so was hungry
and thirsty, and often would spend the whole night in taking care of a
sick patient, so could not have refreshing sleep.

But we never heard him complain of weariness or discomfort.
When he saw his patients suffering, he was very skillful to find ways to
relieve them, and when they recovered he was very happy. He was at
their service night and day. He encouraged and comforted them.
He loved them as children. They wondered much at his kindness to
them, and with their hearts they loved him. I cannot tell all his good
deeds ; it would be impossible. Besides his hospital work he made each
year eight or ten trips into the country, carrying medicines, vaccinating
the people and distributing books and tracts. He went in a Ma-san-
ting (a small native boat) in which he ate and slept, using native and not
foreign food.

Sometimes he would be invited to spend the night with some Chinese
family. I have been with him on these trips, and often after we had
eaten our rice and laid down to sleep, a great crowd would come, pointing
at the door, asking to see him and hear him talk. Although Dr. Kerr
would be tired, he would get up and talk the gospel to them until
they themselves were ready to go away. The next morning he would be
up early, and out hard at work. Sometimes the people would revile
him and throw stones at him, which would be like rain. Once he was
struck in the eye by a stone, and it became swollen up like an egg.

He was then obliged to return with all speed to Canton to be cured.
But he never returned evil for evil ; he never complained, but was always
kind. He sometimes, when out on these trips, would find young men,

He also taught the physicians who were living near the

at the hospital but would send all such patients to them. We urged

This has been their practice ever since.

They not only learned to vaccinate, but afterwards taught their chil-

that within the last thirty years the lives of 100,000 children have been
thus saved through this means. At this time the Doctor was very anxious
to have more room for patients. To us it did not seem as though he could
do more work than he was doing, but he could not bear to have people
who needed treatment go away because the hospital was too small to
receive them.

He went, year by year, to the officials in Canton, and to Hongkong
and Macao, to collect money for its current expenses, and, as he was very
careful in the expenditure of funds, was able each year to lay by some-

up another ward. "Those he had built one storey high he altered to two

obtained help in that way. He put up a chapel which will seat five

He went on building until there were five tiers of wards which

energy, also his love for the Chinese in providing, with such labor, a place

Once he went to Shanghai and was away six weeks. He would, before

In 1876 he left for America. A young man by the name of Carrow took

and he moved up there and took his practice. So I was left with no
Western doctor there. Dr. Carrow would come down once every day to
help. The reputation of the hospital was soon greatly diminished. After
a few months, we heard that the church in America was going to send Dr.
Kerr back, and we rejoiced greatly.

When he arrived he found there was no money in the treasury. He
began with zeal to get funds to go on. Not a year had passed when
the hospital was again prosperous. The Doctor, by his patience and
good judgment, saved the hospital, which had gone down so low while
he was gone. Some years later the business house on Sha-meen, Oliphant
& Co., who had charge of all its funds, failed, and the hospital, with-
out any notice, was left with nothing. It was full of patients, and there
was not a cent to buy anything. Dr. Kerr went to Hongkong, and because
of his great and good name, the foreigners and Chinese there in ten
days had contributed more than had been lost, so he saved the hospital
a second time.

Besides his work at Kuk Fan he had a large out-practice, and for
years, as he had no native physician to send, was obliged to answer all
such calls himself. He often walked, and wherever he went always
carried tracts to give to those whom he might meet, and to the family of
the patients. If he found sick children, beggars or helpless people who
were diseased, he would try to have them carried to the hospital for
treatment. If his patients were poor he never pressed them for money.
He assisted the lepers, giving them books and medicines, also often
money to help them along. He visited the prisons, prescribing for the
inmates who were ill, furnishing medicines, and telling them of the love
of Jesus. I had never known a foreigner who was so humble as to be
such a friend to lepers and prisoners. When the students came to Canton
for the triennial examinations he would get up at three o'clock in the morn-
ing to go out and distribute Christian books among them, coming home
at ten or eleven for breakfast. On Sabbath he would go to the Chinese
service in the morning and then have a Bible class in the afternoon, besides
making his rounds among the patients, so he had no leisure on that day.
Thus he worked on until he was over seventy years old, and then built a
refuge for the insane. There had never been any place in the empire
where anything was done for them. He took them in, and was so kind
to them, and took such good care of them that in the three years he had
them he was able to cure a good many. As I did not work among them
I cannot tell much about this institution. But I did what I could among
my friends and neighbors to get money for it.

This was the Doctor's last good work for this people. Last year in
the 6th month he was taken sick ; medicine could not cure him. The
Father knew he had finished his work, so took him to heaven. When
the Chinese knew he had gone their hearts were filled with sorrow. A
great many followed the coffin to the cemetery. The people in the
neighborhood of the hospital said if they had known when the
procession was going out they would have come out of their houses and
worshipped him as they passed along.

PART II.

At this point Dr. Kerr laid down his pen, never again to
use it in behalf of this people, for whom he had labored for
forty-seven years. As the Master looked upon His servant,
who had wrought so faithfully for Him during a half century,
methinks He said "It is enough." Life's duty done the Father
called him, and joyfully answering the summons he passed into
the skies and now walks with the redeemed in paradise. It seems
not inappropriate to give a short sketch of one whose chapter
on earth is finished, but whom, were our eyes not holden, we
should now see serving Him day and night in His temple.

In a humble home in Adams County, Ohio, on November
30th, 1824, a tiny stranger found a warm welcome. As the grand-
father bent over the new born babe he said : " We will call his
name John," and lifting his hands in prayer over the uncon-
scious infant, he consecrated him at his birth to God. He
prayed that he might so follow the Saviour that the world
should be made better for the life to be lived in it.

The youngest of the family, he became the pet of the
household. A few years later his father concluded to go
further west, to seek a more favorable location and return and
remove his family thither. Full of hope he set out upon this
trip, but, alas ! only a few weeks elapsed ere he was seized with
malarial fever, which proved fatal, and was buried among
strangers. The home was broken up and little John was received
into the family of an uncle residing in Virginia, was soon put
into school and grew up a happy, industrious boy. Always ready
to do a favor, kind and generous to his schoolmates, he gained
many friends among them. Faithful and painstaking in his
studies, full of perseverance and courage, it is not strange his
teachers also loved the boy. In this southern state he was
thrown more or less into association with the colored race, and
his sympathies were greatly enlisted in their behalf. His

When fitted for college he returned to Ohio and entered
Dennison University. Delicate from childhood, and withal
small of stature, he was known among his fellow-students as
"little Kerr." But though left in the background in their
athletic sports, he led the van in his studies. His choice was
the medical profession, and after graduating at the University,
spent two years in Lexington, Kentucky, studying and hearing
lectures, then entered Jefferson University, Pennsylvania, from
which he received his diploma in 1847.

Almost immediately thereafter we find the young M.D.
practicing in a town called Locust Grove, in Southern Ohio.
Scarcely had he become settled when an epidemic of cholera
broke out in a neighboring town, and both the resident physi-
cians died. The young practitioner was sent for, and writes :
"I found a patient in almost every house." The care of these,
in addition to his own practice, " kept me going night and day
almost, but thanks to my Heavenly Father many lives were
saved." Some of these individuals he met when revisiting with
his wife these places in 1892. "You saved me from death by
cholera," and the grasp of the hand, the kindling eye, and
warm welcome, showed they had not forgotten their benefactor.
It gave him great pleasure to see old friends and look once more
upon the familiar scenes of his first years of medical work.
"I was here seven years," he said, "travelling in that time many
miles on horseback with my saddle-bags behind me. We
grew to be pretty well known-my old horse and I." One day
he pointed out his former office, a small building, remarking,
"I slept a good many nights on a counter in that office," add-
ing after a pause, " I never had a father to help me along in the
world." Thus the first years of his medical career were spent.
One day he learned that a Chinese was to deliver a lecture that
evening in the Town Hall, and decided to go and hear him. All
unknown to him this was to be the turning point in his life.

The young man gave an account of the utter ignorance of the
physicians of his native country in all things pertaining to their
profession, of the terrible sufferings of the people on this ac-
count and of the distressing need of properly qualified doctors.
As he listened, he thought why should I not go to China ?
What is there to hinder my doing so? Nothing doubting, he
began the very next day to settle up his business matters pre-
paratory to leaving America. To any word of remonstrance
from relatives or friends there was but one reply. I feel it is
my duty, and a little later (November 14th, 1853), in company
with his wife and Rev. Charles Preston, who was destined for
the same field, this party of three were on their way, "feeling
as though we had said good-by to our native land forever."
Only too true of the young bride, who lived but little more
than two years. After a voyage of nearly six months, " during
which we whiled away a good deal of time studying Greek," they
landed in Canton, where as events proved, these two young men
were to spend their lives. For twenty-three years they worked
hand in hand as brothers beloved, when Mr. Preston passed
away. Twenty-four years later he welcomed his colleague to
heaven. The first letter received from Dr. Kerr, after his
arrival, contains the following extract : "I had not been here
a week before I was in charge of a dispensary, and am receiving
patients every day. Of course my work must be done through
interpreters." A few weeks later, he says: "Have had to dismiss
two of my interpreters, who I found were taking advantage of
my want of knowledge of the language and were not carrying
out my directions in reference to the patients. They had
felt secure in their positions, as they did not suppose I could do
without them." He adds, "Have had no further trouble and
do not expect to have." It did not take his helpers long to
learn that if they expected employment from him they could not
neglect his patients. When the Doctor reached Canton, Dr.
Pelter Parker was in charge of an opthalmic hospital under the
auspices of the Canton Medical Missionary Society, which had
been organized in 1838. In 1855, upon the departure of Dr.

A Character Sketch of J. G. Kerr, M.D., LL.D.

Who has ever visited a forest and not been interested in the variety
and number of its trees from the small sapling to the great oak.

In some respects such a forest represents humanity, where are found
the weak and tender, but right among these frail ones there are also to
be seen those strong and sturdy characters that resemble the mighty oak
in strength and power. Such a character was that of the late Dr. J. G.
Kerr, in whom there was not a particle of weakness. His physical, mental
and moral character all betokened strength of the highest order. One
need not take a second glance at the stalwart form without being
impressed that here was strength and endurance in no unusual degree.

It is no exaggeration to say that Dr. Kerr represented the strong man
among the weak, the giant among the feeble, no matter from what point
his character may be viewed. It was in the early months of 1883 that I
first met him in his own home and in his own hospital, where he did so much
for the alleviation of pain, and the impression received was that he was a
tremendous worker, that he believed in pushing things, the great factor
of success on mission fields. However others might delight in repose
and inactivity, his very nature demanded the opposite-work, incessant
toil, laborious enterprize. This constituted the life of Dr. Kerr, and no
amount of opposition, nor difficulty to be overcome, had the least particle
of influence to swerve him from his purpose when once formed. He
lived to overcome every difficulty, and the reason of his success was not
so much due to his brilliant mind as to his intense application to the
work in hand.

This was what gave him success in whatever he undertook. He first
mastered the situation and then worked to accomplish its end, whether it
was in performing an operation or building a hospital, and this spirit of
work he imparted to others. No one could be with him for any length
of time without catching the same spirit. Dr. Kerr was always at work,
whether at his post of duty or on vacation. He seldom relaxed his
energy in prosecuting his work. Did he find time to go to Hongkong
or Macao for a few days, he spent his leisure moments in investigating
the opium question or in obtaining other valuable information. To
him such investigations were play, and he delighted to solve the problems
they involved.

But if the veteran Doctor believed in work, he also believed in pray-
er. Morning and evening he brought that work to the feet of his Master
for His blessing. It was inspiring to hear him pray for his patients
and the means employed for their recovery. In all his long medical
career in China he never forgot the spiritual needs of his patients, and he
was always found in his place at the prayer meeting and public religious
service, as much as in the operating room.

Through the healing of the body he looked to the healing of
the soul, and no honor conferred upon him ever had a particle of
influence to turn him from his chosen purpose of ministering to men's
bodies that he might reach their souls. Others might have turned aside
to a more lucrative practice where publicity and honor were more
easily gained, but not Doctor Kerr, who was the simple, earnest, self-
sacrificing missionary from the beginning to the end of his career. " We
are missionaries," said he to me, when he spoke of some costly presents
the Chinese wanted to make to him, " and we cannot exhibit anything in

This was preëminently the character of Dr. Kerr. If any one gave
him a present which he considered was not in keeping with his position
as a missionary to have in his parlor, he either refused it, or if that could
not well be done, put it aside where it would not be seen. He did not desire
to have the finest parlor, in the community but the one most befitting
his position as a missionary. How well I remember his words at a prayer
meeting, as he was about to leave on furlough for the home land, nineteen
years ago.

" My work has brought me more honor and praise perhaps than that
usually accorded to the missionary evangelist, but in all my efforts
among the rich and poor I have always had the salvation of my patients at
heart. For this purpose I came to China and for this end have I labored.
Whatever of honor or praise the wealthier class of the Chinese have seen
fit to confer upon me has not, I trust, caused me for a moment to lose sight
of the work to be done in their souls. My interest and sympathy have
always been with the missionary preachers in the preaching of the
simple gospel." For a man who expresses himself thus, it was an easy
matter to gather a class and teach them each Sabbath of the duties of that
religion which Jesus enjoined upon His followers. Another characteris-
tic of Dr. Kerr was his loyalty to his convictions of right, no matter
whether it was connected with a principle of medical practice or that of
church discipline. I cannot forget his long discussions in the newspapers
with some of the Hongkong medical men on the question of the evils of
the opium habit. The former contended that it was a benefit instead of
an evil, and this position he combated with all the might of his strong
nature, and nearly all of the medical practitioners of today uphold him
in the position he took. To him the question ever was, is it right? and
if so he walked therein not counting the cost.

Many others would not have drawn the lance with the Hongkong
English doctors, but he could not let an untruth go unchallenged
before the public and so fought for the truth of his idea wherever he
could. And the same was true of his idea upon the liquor and smoking
habits. He contended they were injurious to the physical, mental, and
moral nature, and although some of his missionary brethren, who in-
dulged in drinking and smoking, did not like his home thrusts on these
themes, yet he continued to plead for total abstinence and non-use of to-
bacco. Many of his friends may have thought him a little severe against
smoking, yet to him it involved a question of right and he could not be
silent when duty demanded that he should speak. The same was true
of his idea of the sacredness of the Christian Sabbath. His practice was
to close the hospital doors on Sunday and not suffer the patients to go
and buy food.

By precept and example he kept the Lord's day. He did not believe
in the missionary traveling on Sunday, nor in selling books to the
natives. I have heard him say that if he were called to travel in the
interior, he would remain over the Sabbath wherever Saturday night
found him. He was also strenuously opposed to native Christians keep-
ing their shops open on Sunday, and believed that Christians and elders
of the church so doing should be expelled. It may he thought that all
these characteristics would make him unbeloved by the Chinese, but this
was not so, and it is a question whether any other physician gained the
love and respect of his patients as much as Dr. Kerr did. He might be
thought brusque at times, yet he was always the friend of the needy and
the downtrodden, and no one knew this better than the Chinese them-
selves, who found in him a wise friend and counsellor. He perhaps did

as much with his cheery words to help his patients as with the use of
drugs. Indeed he himself believed largely in a well regulated diet for
the sick, and what he preached, he practiced. I still remember how he
spent several days or a week in our home, and absolutely refused to eat
anything else but congee. This idea was sometimes very obnoxious to
the Chinese, but still they trusted Dr. Kerr and delighted to chat with
him about their ailments, and he, like a good physician, allowed them to
talk freely in their own way, and when the interview was at an end the
patient always felt satisfied and knew that he had a friend in the Doctor.

It was in these interviews with the Chinese, whether in the sick ward
or in his study, that Dr. Kerr endeared himself to the Chinese and
showed his true nature. Here he unbent and showed his vivacity and
interest in the home life of his patients. He was like another man from
the one who hurried hither and thither to accomplish the daily routine
of his large hospital practice. His witty humor and pleasant words were
a tonic to those who visited him, or sat in his study and heard him talk.
And even when on duty he noticed the children of attending patients or
of those who came to wait upon them, and usually had a smile for young
and old, strong and weak ; for this reason the Chinese rise up to-day and
call him blessed. There was no coarseness in his nature, but the law of
kindness dominated his every day life.

His gentleness and unselfishness were proverbial, and for the young
missionary, he always had a word of cheer, and the new comer always
found in him a friend.

He was wont to say that he did not know Chinese because he did
not commence the study of the language in the right manner. "They
put the Chinese trimetrial classic into my hand," he said, " and that
was no way to learn the language."

Some of us agree with him in this opinion, but his modest confession
that he did not know the Chinese language, was not true, for no
missionary, except a few of the more prominent sinologues, translated
more books into the Chinese language than did Dr. Kerr, and this in a
field where everything was breaking ground and new terms had to be
invented.

Some of these translations may not be perfect, but it was the first
pioneer work done in medicine, and the whole Chinese medical world
owe Dr. Kerr a lasting debt of gratitude for the work he accomplished.
It was no easy task, beside all his other work, to make books which
others had the leisure to make. He must ever be recognised as the
Nestor among medical men in China, for he paved the way for all the
others to follow. In time, no doubt, his many works on medicine will
be superseded by larger and better works, but this will not detract from
the glory and worth of the work done by Dr. Kerr, just as the newer
translations of the Bible do not detract from the work done by Morrison,
Milne, and Bridgman.

His large medical class was also another line of work in which he
was the leader, and although the many students graduated from the
school are not surgeons, nor physicians in the highest sense of that term,
yet these very men are infinitely superior to Chinese quacks, and are
accomplishing a good work for the amelioration of suffering in South
China.

Many of them are doing a noble work, and it is through these very
men that China is becoming acquainted with Western medicine and the
death blow is being dealt to the Chines superstitious ideas of the human
body, and the veto is put upon all Chinese medical learning. In
this general diffusion of medical learning Dr. Kerr was the leading
spirit.

His books have not only been studied by his students, but also by
many of the learned and influential Chinese who have reaped a corres-
ponding benefit from them.

Dr. Kerr was also a man of resources, and this power aided him in
all his work. He knew how to get over a difficulty, and was not confined
to one idea.

I remember once assisting him in an operation that I had never
seen nor read of in the books, and I wondered how he would proceed,
but he did not leave me long to wonder. By a slow and careful process,
he brought light out of darkness and carried the operation to a
successful issue. He was always the careful and conscientious operator,
no matter whether it was upon a poor Chinese farmer, or the U. S.
Minister of Peking. Dr. Kerr's resourceful spirit was also helpful to him
in the building of his large hospital, missionary residence and hospital
church.

He may not have known everything about medicine, building
houses, teaching students, yet he brought order out of chaos and light
out of darkness and success attended all his efforts.

He threw his soul not only into one line of work, but into as many
channels as he possibly could. He was not the man to build up only
one institution at the expense of others, and for this reason he was
always in favor of aiding dispensaries under the charge of good medical
practitioners in the country from the hospital fund. His desire was to
make the hospital at Canton the patron of all the dispensaries in the
interior, to which all the more difficult cases might be sent for treatment.
For this reason he was always glad to aid new dispensaries and especially
the new medical missionaries.

His was not a jealous nature. To help a new missionary in master-
ing the circumstances connected with his medical work was his delight.
For this he lived and wrought. If one door of usefulness was closed, he
immediately found a way to open another. Sometimes when anxious
friends feared that his strength was failing, he rose up again seemingly
as strong as ever. He had reached the forty-third mile stone of his
useful career in China ; when feeling unsatisfied with what had already
been accomplished he founded the first asylum for the insane in China.
True it had been planned long before and much had been done,
but the institution was very weak. It was at this time that Dr. Kerr
took his own money to aid the last child of his creation and godly
enthusiasm. To-day many unfortunate Chinese are being treated for
their maladies.

" Enough," said his friends when he left the great hospital where
he had toiled so long. " Enough," said the world, but not so thought he
and threw himself with all his energy into the building of the Refuge for
Insane. It was only when the work was done and the institution firmly
founded that the summons came to go up higher.

And now he sleeps, not dead, for he lives in the institution he
planted. He came to China in 1854 when all was chaos ; he left it with
one of the largest medical hospitals in China, where men, women, and
children are being treated ; a medical school where young men are fitted
to aid suffering China; an asylum where the poor unfortunate can find
a home; the woman's and children's hospital so recently opened, were
begun under his fostering care. In the old hospital he lives to-day and
in a wider sense than when he first commenced his work. All this
monumental work is due to his intense enthusiasm, absorbing love, and
untiring devotion to the cause of Christ. The rich and poor, the learned
and ignorant, among the Chinese, are ready to venerate him as their ideal
of a medical practitioner. Well is it for us and the cause of Christianity
